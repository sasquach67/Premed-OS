Who makes this
One student · Chapel Hill

I'm a pre-med at UNC. I built HQ because I was running my whole application out of a spreadsheet and a notes app, and neither of them could tell me anything I didn't already type in myself. Every feature here exists because I wanted it at 1am the night before something was due.

It isn't a company. No investors, no ads, no affiliate links, and nothing on this page is sponsored. When a paid resource is the right answer HQ says so and points at the free option first. If it ever needs to cost money, that will be said plainly and long before it happens.

Your grades don't leave your device unless you ask

HQ is local-first. Everything works signed out, stored in your browser. An account adds sync across devices — nothing more. AI features send only what they need, we name every processor that receives anything, and there's a local-only path for the parts you'd rather keep offline. No third-party analytics. No ad tech. Your data is never sold.

What these three are testing

The landing page isn't a wall. Start tracking is primary, Sign in secondary, and the line beside them states the local-first promise outright — that has to be visible before someone decides to bounce, or the whole signed-out design buys you nothing.

The disclaimer is in the hero region, not only the footer (05 §6.1), and it uses the glass chip recipe since it floats over banner art. Beta status is stated top-right rather than implied.

The merge screen is the one to scrutinise. Plain counts of what's on the device, upload as the default, and the safety property stated in the panel rather than a tooltip: local data survives until the server confirms, and a non-empty account gets a reviewable diff. This is the single most likely place in the product to lose someone's work.

The hero leads with the category, then the breadth. Problem-Solution formula: the incumbent is a spreadsheet, so the headline names that. The subhead lands one familiar comparison. The ten chips are the breadth argument, and prose could not carry ten items. They are a wrapping cloud, never a card grid — the moment they grow icons and blurbs they have become the thing this page bans.

Note what's absent: no fabricated testimonials, no user counts, no countdown, no exit popup. The three cards expand three chips rather than repeating them, and each is a claim HQ can actually back.

Honesty gates: the Atlas chip carries soon because Atlas is still a placeholder route. The integrations chip names Canvas but says nothing about grades, because only the calendar-feed path is built. Both stay that way until the code catches up.
